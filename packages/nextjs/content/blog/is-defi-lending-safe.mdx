## What does "safe" mean in DeFi lending?

DeFi lending lets you supply crypto to a smart contract so others can borrow it, or borrow against crypto you post as collateral. DefiLlama counted about $50 billion locked in lending protocols on 6 Nov 2026. Nobody checks your credit, and nobody holds your funds for you: the rules run on a blockchain.

That removes one risk and adds others. No company can gamble with your deposit behind closed doors, because positions are public. In exchange, nobody stands behind the deposit. The FDIC says its insurance covers deposits at insured banks, not crypto assets, and not the failure of crypto companies or losses from theft or fraud.

So "is it safe?" is really three questions. Can the code be trusted? Can what the code relies on be trusted? And can you survive the position you opened?

## What are the main risks of DeFi lending?

| Risk                       | What goes wrong                                      | Who it hits most             | Example                                     |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Liquidation                | Your collateral falls and is sold to repay your debt | Borrowers                    | 10 Oct 2025 crash                           |
| Smart contract bug         | A flaw lets someone take funds                       | Lenders                      | Euler, March 2023                           |
| Oracle failure             | The protocol acts on a wrong price                   | Borrowers and lenders        | Aave wstETH, March 2026; Tectonic, Aug 2026 |
| Bad collateral             | A token used as collateral loses its backing         | Lenders                      | Kelp rsETH, April 2026                      |
| Bad debt                   | A loan ends up worth more than its collateral        | Lenders and backstop stakers | Aave CRV, November 2022                     |
| Locked liquidity           | Everything is lent out, so withdrawals wait          | Lenders                      | Aave, April 2026                            |
| Governance and admin keys  | Someone with control changes the rules or takes over | Everyone                     | Radiant, October 2024                       |
| Website and wallet attacks | A fake site or a bad signature drains your wallet    | Everyone                     | Curve, August 2022                          |

## Why is liquidation the biggest risk for borrowers?

Liquidation is not a failure of DeFi lending; it is how lenders are protected. When your health factor falls below 1.00, anyone can repay part of your debt and take your collateral plus a bonus. It happens without warning, it cannot be undone, and it is the risk a borrower is most likely to meet. [What the Aave health factor is](/blog/aave-health-factor) explains the number, and [how Aave liquidation works](/blog/how-aave-liquidation-works) and [how Morpho liquidation works](/blog/how-morpho-liquidation-works) explain what follows.

Liquidations are also where the big protocols have the longest record. Aave says more than $250 million was liquidated on 10 Oct 2025, when bitcoin fell over 12% in 30 minutes, and $429 million between 31 Jan and 5 Feb 2026, its record in dollar terms, with no downtime in the latter. Its risk provider Chaos Labs counted about $180 million in on-chain liquidations on 10 Oct and about $500,000 of bad debt and expected deficit. So the system held, but each of those liquidations was a real loss for someone.

## Can a smart contract bug take your money?

Yes. Audits lower the odds of a bug, but they do not prove there is none. On 13 Mar 2023 an attacker took almost $200 million from Euler by abusing a flaw in how its lending contracts handled donations and liquidations. Euler had six auditors. Lenders were lucky: the attacker sent back all the funds Euler called recoverable by 3 Apr 2023. That outcome is the exception.

A bug fix can also be a risk of its own. On 4 Nov 2023 a bug report led Aave's Guardian to pause Aave V2 on Ethereum and some assets on V3 networks. For about a week nobody could repay or add collateral on those reserves, as [why an Aave transaction fails](/blog/why-aave-withdraw-or-borrow-fails) describes.

## What happens when a price feed is wrong?

A lending contract cannot see the market. It reads prices from an oracle, and it acts on them whether they are right or not. Three different failures show the range.

- **A configuration error.** On 10 Mar 2026 a mismatch in the settings of Aave's capped wstETH price feed valued wstETH about 2.85% too low on Ethereum Core and Prime. Liquidators took about 10,938 wstETH, around $26 million, from 34 accounts in E-Mode. Those positions were healthy at the real price. No bad debt followed, and the Aave DAO voted to refund the affected users about 512 ETH. A refund is a decision, not a right.
- **A price pushed up on purpose.** On 30 Aug 2026 an attacker inflated TONIC, the thinly traded token of the Tectonic lending protocol on Cronos, and borrowed about $120.4 million against it across nine markets. Validators halted the chain and rolled back nearly two hours of history, which also reversed unrelated transactions, and about $9.19 million still escaped. Mango Markets lost over $100 million the same way on 11 Oct 2022.
- **A price that stops moving.** On 22 Mar 2026 an attacker minted 80 million USR on Resolv with a stolen key, and USR fell to about $0.025 on Curve within 17 minutes. The oracle used by some lending markets still said $1.00, so cheap USR kept borrowing as if it were worth a dollar.

The lesson is to look at which oracle prices your collateral, and how thinly traded that collateral is. Our [guide to stablecoin depegs](/blog/stablecoin-depeg-aave) shows how a capped price feed behaves.

## How can a token you never touched cost you money?

In a shared lending pool, every lender is exposed to every asset accepted as collateral. This is how one of the biggest shocks to DeFi lending in 2026 happened, without a bug in Aave.

On 18 Apr 2026 an attacker exploited Kelp DAO's bridge setup and took about 116,500 rsETH that had no real backing. The attacker then supplied 89,567 rsETH to Aave and borrowed 82,650 WETH, about $191 million. Aave's contracts worked as written; the collateral was the problem. Aave's service providers modelled $124 million to $230 million of bad debt on 20 April, depending on how the loss would be shared. An Aave-led coalition, DeFi United, raised over $300 million in ETH, most of the rsETH was recovered, and Aave restored normal WETH borrowing on 17 May. We found no final loss figure as of 6 Nov 2026.

Other cases followed the same pattern. In November 2025 Stream Finance reported a $93 million loss and its xUSD token collapsed, leaving about $15 million of bad debt on Silo and about $700,000 in one Morpho vault, with no direct impact seen on Aave. Isolated markets limit this kind of spread, because a lender only carries the collateral of the market it chose. [Aave vs Morpho](/blog/aave-vs-morpho) compares the two designs.

## What is bad debt, and who pays for it?

Bad debt is a loan that is worth more than its collateral, so no liquidator has a reason to close it. It usually comes from a fast drop or from an asset too illiquid to sell. In November 2022 a trader borrowed about 40 million CRV from Aave V2 in an attempt to push its price down, and the position left Aave with about $1.6 million of bad debt.

Who pays depends on the protocol. Aave's backstops are Umbrella on Ethereum, where stakers can lose their stake, and the treasury; Aave reported over $250 million in Umbrella and about $200 million in the treasury in February 2026. On Morpho Blue a shortfall is written off against that market's lenders. Either way, a lender's yield is partly payment for carrying this risk.

## Can you always withdraw what you lent?

No. You can only withdraw what has not been lent out. When a market reaches 100% utilization, withdrawals wait until borrowers repay or new deposits arrive. Rates rise steeply at that point to pull money back, as [how Aave interest rates work](/blog/aave-interest-rates) explains, but that takes time.

This is exactly what happens in a panic. Users pulled about $8.45 billion from Aave within two days of the Kelp exploit, and major pools hit 100% utilization, so some lenders could not leave right away. After the Resolv exploit, four Morpho markets reached 100% utilization with about $7.77 million locked.

## Who can change the rules?

Read who holds control before you deposit. On Aave, governance and risk stewards can change parameters while your loan is open. A Morpho Blue market's rules are fixed when it is created, but a Morpho vault's curator decides which markets your deposit goes to. Neither model removes trust; each one places it in different people.

Admin keys matter as much as code. In October 2024 attackers planted malware on developers' devices at Radiant, a lending protocol, got three approvals from the team's multisig, took ownership of a core contract and drained more than $50 million from its pools on Arbitrum and BNB Chain.

## Can the website or your wallet be the weak point?

Yes, and audited contracts do not help here. On 9 Aug 2022 attackers hijacked Curve's domain and showed a copy of the site, and users who approved its contract lost about $570,000. On 11 Jul 2024 Compound's site redirected to a malicious page in a similar attack, while the protocol's contracts and funds stayed safe.

Use bookmarks rather than search results, read what your wallet asks you to sign, and remove token approvals you no longer need.

## Is DeFi lending safer than a centralized crypto lender?

It is a different risk, not simply a smaller one. On a centralized platform your risk is the company. The FTC alleged that Celsius told customers they could withdraw at any time and that it was safer than a bank, while it had made $1.2 billion of unsecured loans and misappropriated more than $4 billion in deposits. It filed for bankruptcy in July 2022.

In DeFi nobody can make a secret loan with your deposit, and anyone can inspect collateral and debt on-chain. But you carry the risks in this guide yourself, and nobody is obliged to make you whole. [Crypto-backed loans explained](/blog/crypto-backed-loans) compares the options for borrowers.

## How can you lower these risks?

If you borrow:

- Keep a real margin. [What a safe Aave health factor is](/blog/safe-aave-health-factor) shows how to pick one for the time you need to react.
- Prefer deep, widely traded collateral, and know which oracle prices it.
- Keep something ready to repay with: a stablecoin balance beats selling in a crash.
- Don't assume a stablecoin, a wrapped token or a staking token always trades at its peg.

If you lend:

- Remember that yield pays you for risk. A rate far above the market is a question, not a gift.
- Check which collateral your deposit can be lent against, and who chose it.
- Split deposits across protocols, and don't rely on being able to leave in a crisis.
- Read the terms if you buy cover: it applies to named protocols and events. Nexus Mutual had no active cover for USR when Resolv was exploited.

This guide describes risks; it is not financial advice.

## How can alerts help?

An alert covers one risk in this list: a health factor that falls while you are away. Tokenping checks your Aave and Morpho positions every 5 minutes and, while a health factor is below the threshold you set, messages you every 15 minutes on Signal, Telegram or email. That gives you time to repay or add collateral.

It does not protect you from a hack, a frozen market or a price that jumps between two checks. You can [check your Aave positions](/aave) or [your Morpho positions](/morpho) without paying; alerts cost <AlertPrice />.
